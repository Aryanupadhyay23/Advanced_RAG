Distance & Similarity

Core mathematical tools for comparing vectors – Euclidean distance, Manhattan distance, cosine similarity, and dot product.

1. Core Idea

  • What are they? Functions that quantify how close (or similar) two vectors are in a vector space. They are the mathematical foundation of nearest‑neighbour search, clustering, and information retrieval.
  • Why do they matter? In ML and NLP, everything is a vector (embeddings, features). Comparing these vectors reliably determines retrieval quality, classification, and clustering performance.
  • Distance metrics (Euclidean, Manhattan) produce larger values for less similar vectors. Similarity metrics (cosine, dot product) produce larger values for more similar vectors.
  • Choice depends on: vector normalization, sparsity, sensitivity to magnitude, and the geometry of the embedding space.

2. Formulas & Intuition

Let two vectors be $\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$.

2.1 Euclidean Distance ($L^2$ norm)

$ d_{\text{Euclidean}}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (u_i - v_i)^2 } $

The straight‑line distance in multi‑dimensional space. Generalization of the Pythagorean theorem.

Emphasises large differences (squared). Sensitive to both direction and magnitude. If vectors are unit‑normalized, Euclidean distance relates to cosine similarity: $ \|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|^2 = 2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})) $.

2.2 Manhattan Distance ($L^1$ norm)

$ d_{\text{Manhattan}}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |u_i - v_i| $

Also called city‑block distance. Sum of absolute differences along each coordinate.

Less sensitive to outliers than Euclidean (no squaring). Preferable in high‑dimensional spaces where Euclidean distance can become uninformative (curse of dimensionality). Often used with sparse vectors.

2.3 Cosine Similarity

$ \text{cosine}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{ \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} }{ \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| } = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i }{ \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i^2} } $

Measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors. Range: $[-1, 1]$. Vectors pointing in the same direction have similarity $1$; orthogonal vectors $0$; opposite directions $-1$. In NLP, embeddings are often in the positive orthant, so range typically $[0,1]$.

Magnitude‑invariant: captures similarity of direction, not length. Dominant metric for comparing text embeddings (Word2Vec, BERT, etc.).

2.4 Dot Product (Inner Product)

$ \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i $

The simplest similarity measure. No normalization; sensitive to both direction and magnitude. Equivalent to cosine similarity if vectors are unit‑normalized: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ when $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| = 1$.

Used in neural networks (logits, attention scores) and often in maximum inner product search (MIPS) for recommendation systems. Large dot product indicates both alignment and large magnitudes.

4. Geometric Intuition

🔹 Euclidean vs Manhattan

Euclidean is the direct “as the crow flies” distance. Manhattan is the path along grid lines – like walking through city blocks. Manhattan accumulates more distance for diagonal movement.

In a 2D plane with points (0,0) and (3,4): Euclidean = 5, Manhattan = 7. The difference grows with dimension and off‑axis displacement.

🔹 Cosine vs Dot Product

Cosine ignores vector length – it measures whether two documents talk about the same topic, even if one is a long essay and the other a tweet. Dot product rewards both topical similarity and verbosity. So a long document about cats might have a larger dot product with a short query than a short document about cats – which may be undesirable.

5. Interview Questions

Q1: When would you use cosine similarity over Euclidean distance?

A: When you care about the orientation of vectors, not their magnitude. Typical in NLP with embeddings, because document length shouldn’t dominate similarity. Cosine is also preferred in high‑dimensional spaces where Euclidean distances become unreliable.

Q2: Prove that for unit vectors, Euclidean distance and cosine similarity are directly related.

A: For unit vectors $\|\mathbf{u}\|=\|\mathbf{v}\|=1$, we have $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2 \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 2 - 2\cos(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})$. Thus $\cos(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|^2$. Minimising Euclidean distance is equivalent to maximising cosine similarity.

Q3: Why might Manhattan distance perform better than Euclidean in high dimensions?

A: In high dimensions, the volume of space expands such that Euclidean distances between random points tend to become very similar (concentration). Manhattan distance, not squaring differences, is less sensitive to this effect and can retain more contrast. It’s also more robust to outliers.

Q4: If you have normalized embeddings (all vectors on the unit sphere), does the choice between cosine and dot product matter?

A: No. For unit‑norm vectors, dot product and cosine similarity are identical (since $\|\mathbf{u}\|=\|\mathbf{v}\|=1$). In practice, many embedding models (e.g., text‑embedding‑ada‑002) produce normalized vectors, so dot product suffices.

Q5: In a vector database using cosine similarity, how is the search typically implemented efficiently?

A: Cosine similarity is equivalent to inner product after $L^2$ normalization. Vector databases pre‑normalize all vectors and then use maximum inner product search (MIPS) or approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) algorithms like HNSW, IVF, or Faiss with inner‑product metric. This is fast and well‑optimized.

6. Common Mistakes

❌ Wrong Belief✅ Correct Understanding
“Cosine similarity measures distance.”It measures similarity; higher is more similar. To get a distance, use $1 - \text{cosine}$ or $\arccos(\text{cosine})$.
“Dot product and cosine similarity are the same.”Only equal when vectors are unit‑normalized. Otherwise, magnitude heavily influences the dot product.
“Euclidean distance is always better because it’s the ‘true’ distance.”It’s only one metric; many applications benefit from magnitude‑invariant metrics. The “best” metric depends on the data and task.
“Cosine similarity can handle vectors of any dimension without issue.”While cosine is robust, very high dimensions still suffer from the “hubness” problem where some points become universal nearest neighbours. Dimensionality reduction or careful model choice may still be needed.
“Manhattan distance is always slower than Euclidean.”Both are $O(n)$ for dimension $n$; Manhattan avoids a square root, making it slightly faster per comparison.

7. Exam Cram Facts (Day‑Before Revision)

  1. Euclidean: $d = \sqrt{\sum (u_i - v_i)^2}$ – straight‑line distance, sensitive to outliers.
  2. Manhattan: $d = \sum |u_i - v_i|$ – grid‑walk distance, robust to outliers.
  3. Cosine: $\frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\|\|v\|}$ – direction‑only, range $[-1,1]$, standard for text embeddings.
  4. Dot product: $u \cdot v = \sum u_i v_i$ – includes magnitude; equals cosine if vectors are unit‑norm.
  5. For unit vectors: $\text{cosine} = \text{dot}$, and $\|u-v\|^2 = 2(1-\text{cosine})$.
  6. In high dimensions, Euclidean suffers from distance concentration; cosine is more reliable.
  7. Vector DBs use cosine by normalizing and then doing MIPS (inner product).
  8. Distance metrics convert to similarity via negation or $1 - \text{similarity}$.
